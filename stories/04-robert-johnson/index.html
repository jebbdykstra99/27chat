Robert Johnson's Work: The 1936–37 Sessions, 29 Songs, and the Crossroads Legend

By Jebb Dykstra, Juno, Vesta and Iris

27chat.com · Story 04 · The work didn't stop.

Robert Johnson left 29 songs from two Texas sessions. This story follows the work, then sets the legends beside the record.

Who was Robert Johnson?

Robert Johnson was a Mississippi Delta blues singer and guitarist whose 1936–37 recordings became among the most influential in blues and rock. He was born in Hazlehurst, Mississippi, around 1911; the often-cited May 8 birthdate is disputed because census, school and marriage records disagree (Handbook of Texas). He learned from Charley Patton, Son House and Willie Brown, then studied for a year or longer around 1930–31 with guitarist Ike Zimmerman (Library of Congress).

When and where did Robert Johnson record?

Johnson recorded at two sessions run by producer Don Law for the American Record Corporation (ARC): San Antonio in November 1936 and Dallas in June 1937. At San Antonio's Gunter Hotel he cut eight sides on November 23, one on November 26 and seven the next day; at 508 Park Avenue in Dallas, three on June 19 and ten on June 20 (Handbook of Texas). "Terraplane Blues" was his hit, released in March 1937 (LOC).

How many songs did Robert Johnson record?

Johnson recorded 29 songs in 41 takes, about two hours of music, according to the Library of Congress.

What made Robert Johnson's guitar playing different?

Johnson often played to a pulsing 4/4 "big beat," the meter big-band rhythm sections and later rock bands built on (LOC). He also learned songs from pianists Leroy Carr and Peetie Wheatstraw (LOC). His arrangements fit the roughly two-and-a-half-minute 78 with few retakes (Handbook of Texas). His musical associate Johnny Shines said in the 1990 film The Search for Robert Johnson: "You can't hear a blues tune or a rock tune that don't have some of Robert's chords in it" (Observer, 2011).

How did Robert Johnson influence Muddy Waters, Clapton and the Rolling Stones?

His songs first lived on through protégés who covered them: Robert Lockwood, Johnny Shines, Muddy Waters and Howlin' Wolf (LOC). In Britain, the 1961 LP King of the Delta Blues Singers reached Eric Clapton and the Rolling Stones' Brian Jones and Keith Richards (LOC). Clapton recorded "Cross Road Blues" with Cream and made a 14-song tribute, Me and Mr. Johnson, in 2004 (Handbook of Texas).

What are King of the Delta Blues Singers and The Complete Recordings?

King of the Delta Blues Singers was a 1961 Columbia LP of 16 takes compiled by Frank Driggs. The Complete Recordings (1990) gathered all 41 (LOC). The 1990 set sold to about 600,000 buyers in its first year, ten times expectations (LOC), won a 1991 Grammy (Handbook of Texas) and entered the National Recording Registry in 2003. A second King volume is dated 1969 by the LOC and 1965 by the Handbook of Texas. The Rock and Roll Hall of Fame inducted him in its first class, 1986, as an Early Influence (Rock Hall archive; Handbook of Texas).

What do the two known photos of Robert Johnson show?

Two photographs are accepted as authentic: a photo-booth self-portrait with a cigarette at his mouth, and a Hooks Bros. studio portrait from Memphis in a hat and pinstriped suit (Vanity Fair, 2008). 27chat describes them and does not reproduce them. Disputed: The estate said a third photo had been authenticated in 2013. In 2015, 49 historians and musicians, including Gayle Dean Wardlow and David Evans, found no substantial evidence that it shows him (Guardian, 2015).

Did Robert Johnson sell his soul? The legends and the record

No firsthand account has Johnson claiming a deal with the devil; de Vos finds the claim always attributed to a friend of a friend. The Handbook of Texas, by contrast, says he often claimed he learned guitar from the Devil.

Legend: the crossroads deal

Origin: The crossroads story was at first most commonly associated with Tommy Johnson, no relation. His brother LeDell's account, in David Evans's 1971 biography, has a figure at a midnight crossroads tune the guitar and hand it back (de Vos, 2011). How it grew: Pete Welding's 1966 Down Beat essay wrote that Son House suggested Johnson had sold his soul. Welding never published a full House interview, and Julius Lester's earlier one has no devil (de Vos). Greil Marcus (1975) and Robert Palmer (1981) built it up, and the film Crossroads spread it (de Vos). Wardlow concluded the myth "has no single source" (de Vos). The record: The LOC credits a year or longer of study with Zimmerman.

Legend: the poisoning

Origin: In 1942, a woman introduced to Alan Lomax as Johnson's mother said he had been poisoned; Lomax published the account in 1993 (Skeptical Inquirer, 2025). Welding's 1966 essay quoted Johnny Shines blaming a woman, and it reported that Son House had heard three versions (Skeptical Inquirer, citing Welding). How it grew: Bruce Conforth and Wardlow's Up Jumped the Devil (2019) names a man they say admitted it; Skeptical Inquirer's Brett Taylor (2025) says the accounts conflict and calls the confession unproven. The record: Wardlow found the death certificate in 1968 and its back in 1996. It lists no attending physician and records a plantation owner's opinion that the cause was syphilis (Rolling Stone, 2001), a note Evans has called racist (Skeptical Inquirer). The cause is not established. He died near Greenwood in August 1938, on the 16th per the Handbook of Texas or the 13th per the Rock Hall archive.

Disputed: the three graves

The claims: Payne Chapel M.B. Church at Quito got the first marker in 1990, from an Atlanta rock band (Mississippi Blues Trail). At Mount Zion M.B. Church near Morgan City, Skip Henderson's Mt. Zion Memorial Fund and Columbia unveiled an obelisk on April 20, 1991, a cenotaph placed because the certificate lists "Zion Church" (Mt. Zion Memorial Fund). After 1996, Rosie Eskridge led Wardlow to Little Zion M.B. Church, where she said her husband dug the grave (Rolling Stone). The Blues Trail dates Stephen LaVere's headstone there to 2002; Rolling Stone reported one planned for August 2001. The record: The 2007 Blues Trail marker calls Little Zion the "most likely" site, which is not proof.

Legend: the hellhound and the devil

Origin: On June 20, 1937, Johnson recorded "Hellhound on My Trail" and "Me and the Devil Blues" (Handbook of Texas). How it grew: Welding titled his essay after the first. Driggs opened the 1961 LP with "Cross Road Blues," which drew listeners to devil and crossroads imagery (de Vos, citing Waide, 2009). The record: Literally, "Cross Road Blues" is about a man at a crossroads at sundown, trying to flag a ride and asking the Lord for mercy (de Vos, citing Palmer). The imagery is in the songs; the deal is not in the record.
