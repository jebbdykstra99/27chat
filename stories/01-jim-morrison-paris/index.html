How Jim Morrison Came to Rest in Paris

27chat.com · Story 01 · The work didn't stop.

In March 1971, Jim Morrison left Los Angeles with a Doors album nearly finished and a different kind of work in mind. Ray Manzarek later remembered the announcement as almost casual: "I'm off to Paris." The band was left to complete L.A. Woman without its singer, and Morrison flew out to join his partner, Pamela Courson, who had already found an apartment in the Marais. According to the Independent, he arrived on March 11.

Manzarek, looking back in a 2014 Classic Rock interview, described what the trip was supposed to mean: Morrison "was going to write his poetry and rejuvenate himself," following Hemingway, Fitzgerald and Henry Miller as an American writer in Paris. Leaving was also a way out of a hard stretch at home. In 1970 a Miami court had convicted him of indecent exposure and profanity after a concert there, and he was free on bail while he appealed.

The work in Paris

Those last months produced writing, not records. Morrison filled notebooks. One of them, a green composition book with "Paris Journal" written across its cover, appeared at Julien's Auctions in 2020. Fine Books & Collections reported that it opens with the lines "so much forgotten already / so much forgotten / so much to forget," which continue over the following fourteen double-sided pages. It came from the box of manuscripts Pamela Courson kept after his death. The magazine also noted that the dating to his final months comes partly from how "the legend goes."

When UPI reported his death, a record-industry spokesman said Morrison had been in Paris "writing a book." That is the frame this series keeps: the work didn't stop.

July 3, 1971

The French death certificate records that James Douglas Morrison, "writer," died on July 3, 1971, at 17 rue Beautreillis. Courson told police she found him in the bathtub early that morning. The doctor who examined the body reported no sign of trauma and concluded that death was caused by cardiac arrest, a natural death. No autopsy was performed. As the Guardian noted on the 50th anniversary, French law did not require one at the time.

The news came out slowly. Manager Bill Siddons later said it had been kept quiet "to avoid the notoriety and circus-like atmosphere" that had surrounded other recent rock deaths. He also said Morrison had seen a Paris doctor about a respiratory problem and had complained of it the day he died.

Because there was no autopsy, other accounts have circulated. In his 2007 book The End: Jim Morrison, Sam Bernett, former manager of the Rock 'n' Roll Circus nightclub, claimed Morrison actually died at the club and was carried home afterward. The BBC reported that Paris prosecutors said they were unlikely to re-examine the case. Biographer Stephen Davis told the BBC that if Bernett's version were true, "it would have come out before now." The claim has never been proven. Neither has the rumor that Morrison faked his death. Manzarek said the sealed coffin "was obviously going to give flame to an insane rumour" that he was still alive. Asked whether he had any doubt that Morrison is buried at Père Lachaise, he answered: "None. I never did."

Père Lachaise, July 7, 1971

Morrison was buried at Père Lachaise on Wednesday, July 7, 1971. The American Embassy described it as a private ceremony attended by only a few close friends. He lies in division 6 of a cemetery that also holds Oscar Wilde, Molière, Balzac and Edith Piaf, a crowd of writers he would have recognized.

The grave started out plain. In 1981, for the tenth anniversary of his death, Croatian sculptor Mladen Mikulin set a white marble bust on the tomb. Over seven years, visitors covered it in graffiti and chipped away its nose for souvenirs, and in 1988 it disappeared. In May 2025, Paris police announced that they had found it by chance during a search in an unrelated fraud investigation. The cemetery's curator said then that he did not know whether it would come back.

The bust was replaced by something quieter. Morrison's family installed a flat stone with a bronze plaque bearing his name, his dates and a Greek line, ΚΑΤΑ ΤΟΝ ΔΑΙΜΟΝΑ ΕΑΥΤΟΥ. It is usually translated "true to his own spirit." Some sources date the stone to 1990 and others to 1991. Newsweek reported in May 1991 that the parents had placed it "only a few months ago."

Managing a pilgrimage

The visitors kept coming, and the cemetery has had to manage them. By 2004 Père Lachaise had hired a security guard to watch the grave, and the conservator told Reuters that families of people buried nearby had petitioned to have Morrison's remains moved. On April 15, 2004, according to ethnologist Peter Jan Margry, the authorities closed off the grave with a ring of anchored iron barriers. In Margry's 2008 account, visitors still brought flowers, notes and poems but stood back from the stone, guards kept watch, and objects left behind were usually cleared away. Fans have kept gathering on July 3. On the 50th anniversary, the Guardian reported, one of them recited Morrison's poems at the graveside.
