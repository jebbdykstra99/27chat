Amy Winehouse's Music: Frank, Back to Black, Lioness and the Legends

By Jebb Dykstra, Juno, Vesta and Iris

27chat.com · Story 02 · The work didn't stop.

Amy Winehouse made two studio albums, Frank (2003) and Back to Black (2006), and both were built on jazz and the soul records she loved. This story follows that work: the influences, the sessions, the awards, the last recording, and what was left after her death on July 23, 2011.

What were Amy Winehouse's jazz influences on Frank?

Her favourite singers were Sarah Vaughan, Dinah Washington and Minnie Riperton. "I would say that jazz is my own language," she told the Guardian in February 2004, from her flat in Camden. She had been listening to Ella Fitzgerald since she was 11, and hearing Vaughan made her realise "that a whisper can be so much more effective than just belting something out." She loved Washington because "she would sing all over the song, rather than just do it straight."

Island Records released Frank in 2003. In its October review, the Guardian placed her "somewhere between Nina Simone and Erykah Badu." Salaam Remi, one of the album's producers, first met her in Miami, where she sat down and sang "The Girl from Ipanema." The label wanted hip-hop beats under the songs, he said in 2012, "but musically she was really in a jazz zone, she was writing jazz."

Who produced Back to Black, and how was it recorded?

Mark Ronson and Salaam Remi produced Back to Black, and members of the Dap-Kings played on Ronson's tracks. Remi said she brought him her new love of the Shangri-Las, doo-wop and soul, then took the same idea to Ronson: "This is how I want the chords." Ronson sketched a track for "Back to Black." The next day, he told the FADER, she said, "That's what I want my whole album to sound like." When he asked her to change a chorus line that didn't rhyme, she answered, "Why would I fix that? That's what came out."

Most of the band tracks for Ronson's songs were cut at Daptone's analogue studio in Brooklyn. Mix engineer Tom Elmhirst told Sound on Sound in 2007 (as quoted by MusicRadar) that drums, piano, guitar and bass were recorded together in one room, with the drums on a single microphone. Strings and brass were added in London. Ronson called the production "a Trojan Horse": "that's not why the record is what it is. It's her."

How many Grammys did Amy Winehouse win?

She won six. Five came at the 50th Grammy Awards in 2008: Record of the Year, Song of the Year and Best Female Pop Vocal Performance for "Rehab," Best Pop Vocal Album for Back to Black, and Best New Artist. She performed by satellite from London because her US visa was approved too late. The sixth was a Grammy shared with Tony Bennett in 2012.

What was Amy Winehouse's last recording?

Her last recording was "Body and Soul," a duet with Tony Bennett made at Abbey Road in March 2011 for his album Duets II. The Guardian reported that the first thing she said on meeting one of her heroes was "I'm not going to cry." The duet won Best Pop Duo/Group Performance, and Bennett invited her parents to accept it with him.

How did Amy Winehouse die?

She died of alcohol poisoning at her home in Camden, north London, on July 23, 2011. She was 27. In October the St Pancras coroner recorded a verdict of misadventure, and the BBC reported the inquest heard she had given up drugs but kept returning to drink after weeks of abstaining.

Amy Winehouse legends and disputed stories

These stories have circulated for years. Each is labeled here as legend or dispute and set next to what is on the record.

Legend: "Amy Winehouse refused rehab"

The legend: the song turned one refusal into the defining story about her. Grammy.com (2017) says "Rehab" "firmly established her iconoclastic legend." The record: Grammy.com (2017) traces the song to 2005, when her first manager, Nick Shymansky, urged her to go to rehab. She said she would go if her father thought it was necessary, and he told her he didn't think she needed treatment. Ronson told Billboard (2015, via Rolling Stone) that she told the story so lightly he encouraged her to write it. The dispute: Asif Kapadia's 2015 documentary Amy presents the episode much as the song does. Mitch Winehouse told RTÉ Radio 1 (July 10, 2015) that he had actually said she didn't need rehab "at that point," meaning 2005, and that he later took her "in and out of rehab and detox." Kapadia told the Guardian (June 27, 2015) that the first time he interviewed Mitch, he didn't say "at the time."

Legend: "a lost third album"

Where it comes from: a 2015 Billboard feature, reported by Rolling Stone on July 1, 2015. In it, Remi said she had probably finished writing a few weeks before she died ("we had 14 songs"), and Rolling Stone reported she had booked studio time with him and Ronson. The record: in the same feature, Universal Music UK chief David Joseph said he had destroyed her demos: "It was a moral thing." No third album exists. What was released is Lioness. Its tracks include "Between the Cheats," which the Foundation says was recorded in 2008 "for potential inclusion on album three."

Disputed: her final years

The dispute: Amy portrays a decline played out in front of paparazzi and online audiences; the Guardian's 2015 piece on the film points to YouTube footage of the Belgrade concert. Mitch Winehouse told RTÉ the film was "a lie," and said she "quit drugs three years before she died" and was "surrounded by people who loved her." The record: the inquest found that no illegal drugs were in her system.

What is Lioness: Hidden Treasures, and what is the Amy Winehouse Foundation?

Lioness: Hidden Treasures is her posthumous album, released on December 5, 2011 and compiled by Remi and Ronson with her family, her management and Island. Its twelve tracks include unreleased songs, the original ballad version of "Tears Dry," a cover of "A Song for You," the Leon Russell song made famous by Donny Hathaway, and the Bennett duet. It went to No. 1 in the UK, selling over 194,000 copies in its first week. In the UK, £1 from each copy went to the Amy Winehouse Foundation, which launched on September 14, 2011, what would have been her 28th birthday. The Foundation says it helps young people manage their emotional wellbeing.

By the time of the inquest, the BBC noted, Back to Black had become the UK's bestselling album of the 21st century.
